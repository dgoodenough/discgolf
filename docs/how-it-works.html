How the DGPT points system works

← back to the forecast

The Disc Golf Pro Tour crowns its season champion at the Powerball Cup (the DGPT Championship). Who gets to play in it is decided by the Powerball DGPT World Standings — a season-long points race. This page explains how the points work, what counts, and how a player gets in.

1 · Every event awards points by finishing place

Each points event awards points on a fixed curve: the winner gets the most, and every place after that gets a bit less, and every finisher earns something (FPO points bottom out at 1 from 50th place, MPO from 125th). Bigger events award more — the same curve is scaled up by event class:

Event classWin awardsScale
DGPT (Elite Series)150base curve
DGPT+200base × 4⁄3
DGPT Playoffs250base × 5⁄3
PDGA Major300base × 2
Doubles Championship137.5each partner gets half the combined points of the two singles places their team finish spans
JomezPro Series20flat bonus: 20 for a win, 10 for top 5, 5 for top 10

Ties split the pot: everyone in a tie gets the average of the points for the places the tie covers (two players tied 2nd each get the average of 2nd- and 3rd-place points).

2 · Not every finish counts

A player's season total isn't everything they earned — each class of event has its own cap, so skipping events (or having a bad one) eventually stops hurting:

ClassWhat counts
DGPT + DGPT+ (incl. doubles)best 10 finishes (of ~15 events)
Playoffs (GMC + MVP Open)both count, always
Majorsbest 2 — MPO has 3 eligible majors, FPO has 4
JomezPro bonusall of it, on top

That's 14 counted finishes plus bonus, for a theoretical maximum of 2,800 points. Wrinkles: the Heinola Open awards no FPO points (it ends four days before the U.S. Women's Championship), and the USDGC awards no points because it falls after the points season ends at the MVP Open. In the forecast's expanded player view, finishes that don't count are struck through.

3 · How you make the Powerball Cup

The Cup field is 32 MPO / 20 FPO. Three ways in:

  • Automatic bid — finish the season top 28 MPO / top 18 FPO in the standings.
  • Playoff run — the top 4 MPO / 2 FPO finishers at the MVP Open who missed the standings cut take the last spots.
  • Winner's invite — win any DGPT Elite or Major singles event and you're in regardless, added as a bottom seed without bumping anyone (doubles and Jomez wins don't qualify).

The playoffs themselves are gated by the standings, both read at the same moment — where the table stood after Pro Worlds, the last major of the season. The Green Mountain Championship field is the top 100 MPO / 50 FPO (expanding to 120/60 if it doesn't fill), and the MVP Open field is the top 72/36, plus the top 8/4 GMC finishers from outside that line. So a hot September can carry a player from 90th place all the way into the Cup.

Neither field opens all at once — each is invited in waves, and every wave that opens stays open:

OpensEventWho
Mar 23GMCFull Tour Card holders rated 1010+ MPO / 930+ FPO
Aug 13GMCStandings wave 1 — top 60 MPO / 30 FPO
Aug 13MVP OpenTier 1 — top 50 MPO / 25 FPO (post-Ledgestone)
Sep 1GMCStandings wave 2 — top 100 MPO / 50 FPO
Sep 1MVP OpenTier 2 — top 72 MPO / 36 FPO (post-Worlds)
Sep 20MVP OpenDecided at GMC — the top 8 MPO / 4 FPO finishers from outside the tier lines

That last row is a wave you win rather than one you are invited to, and it is the reason the MVP Open's field cannot be settled early: however complete its entry list looks in September, the players who take those spots are not known until GMC has been played.

The forecast reads the published signup lists and takes them literally: a player who has entered is in that field in every simulated season, wherever the standings finish — which is how a tour-card holder ranked outside the top 100 ends up playing GMC. A player who has not entered still gets in on points, because the waves that would reach them haven't opened yet. That uncertainty shrinks with every wave.

4 · What a seed is worth

The standings don't only decide who plays the Cup — they decide what score each qualifier starts on. The Powerball Cup is four rounds of stroke play at Ivy Hill, Lynchburg, Virginia, Oct 15–18, and every player tees off on a starting score set by their World Standings position. A season spent climbing the table is carried onto the first tee:

The two ladders are the same ladder, FPO's one stroke lower throughout — which is 2026's single change to the format: both top seeds gave up a stroke from 2025's −8 and −7. Everyone who arrives from outside the standings cut is a bottom seed at even: the four MPO / two FPO wildcards out of the MVP Open, and any event winner playing on the special invite. The tour's wider format review is due to be announced later this year, for 2027.

So the forecast publishes the ladder as its own column. Starting strokes runs each simulated season's finishing position through the table above and shows the distribution of the score that player would tee off on, with a last bar for the seasons they never make the field at all — a qualification picture and a seeding picture in one. The finish-distribution sparkline it replaces is still there under Advanced, alongside it.

And once the strokes are known, the Cup can be played. Win Cup is the championship itself, simulated: in every season where a player reaches Lynchburg, their four rounds are drawn from their rating the same way every other event on this site is drawn, the seed's head start is added to the total, and the lowest score wins. Read it against Cup next to it — Cup is a question each player answers alone, so 32 of them can all sit at 100%; Win Cup is a race, so the whole field's odds add to 100% instead. It is the one number here that says a stroke of seeding is worth exactly a stroke, rather than assuming the best player wins.

This is also why a seeding battle between two players who are already locked in is still a battle, and why Biggest Movers tracks projected final seed next to Cup odds: once qualification odds hit 100%, position is the only thing left to win. And the week is worth more than any other on tour — a record $350,000 purse, $50,000 of it to each of the MPO and FPO winners.

5 · The Worlds play-in

Pro Worlds fills most of its field on tour and PDGA criteria and leaves a few spots open. Those are decided at a one-round play-in the week of the event: the top 6 MPO and 2 FPO advance into the Worlds field. The play-in awards no World Standings points itself, so it has no row in the schedule — but Worlds is a major, so winning through it is a door into 300-point finishes. Players on the entry list who missed direct qualification carry a play-in flag on the Worlds row in their expanded view, showing how often they win a spot. It is raced against the whole entry list, entrants with no World Standings row included, so those spots stay as scarce in the model as they are on the course — and the two divisions look nothing alike. The 2026 MPO play-in drew 53 players, nine of them with a standings row and none of them near the Cup; the FPO play-in drew five, all five ranked, competing for two spots.

6 · What the forecast does with all this

The forecast simulates the rest of the season 100,000 times: each player's scores are drawn from their PDGA rating, fields come from real registration lists (or participation patterns where registration isn't open, or the qualification rules above where a field is invited rather than entered), live events are projected from the holes actually remaining, and every rule above is applied to every simulated season. The percentages you see are just how often each outcome happened. Full methodology and source: github.com/dgoodenough/discgolf.

7 · The experimental tabs: 2027, What if, and the EuroTour

Everything above describes a season being played. The 2027 and EuroTour tabs describe two that have not started, and they are a different kind of thing — a projection built out of a schedule announcement rather than a forecast built out of results. The What if tab is a third thing again: it drops a player who does not exist into the 2027 field. All three are labelled experimental on the page for a reason, and it is worth knowing exactly which parts are facts and which are ours.

What is real. The calendar. The DGPT published the 2027 schedule: every event, its designation, its host city and its dates. Round counts are read off those dates — a three-day stop plays three rounds, Pro Worlds' five-day window five — which is the rule every 2026 event obeys. Three structural changes are real too, and they are the interesting part: the postseason moves behind the USDGC, which enters the MPO points race for the first time as a fourth major; the Playoffs move to Ivy Hill and the Kansas City Wide Open, with the Cup in Austin; and the JomezPro Series grows from three stops to seven, which quietly makes its all-counting bonus pool one of the larger terms in a touring pro's season total.

What is assumed on the 2027 tab. That the points structure does not change: same per-place curves, same class multipliers, same per-class counting caps (best 10 DGPT, both playoffs, best 2 majors, all JomezPro bonus), same playoff qualification windows and the same Cup seed ladder, with Ivy Hill inheriting the Green Mountain Championship's rules and the Kansas City Wide Open the MVP Open's. That players carry today's ratings into next season unchanged. And that each of them plays the same share of US stops, European stops and JomezPro stops as they played this year — applied to a bigger calendar, so more starts rather than the same ones. The weakest single assumption is the USDGC: it is an invitational whose 2027 invitation rules are not published, and it is modelled here like any other US stop.

The EuroTour's scoring is published, and it is used as published. Eight events across three points categories, each paying a fixed amount for a win and counting a fixed number of results:

Cat.WinEventsCountedMax
1250European Disc Golf Championships, European Openbest 1 of 2250
2200both European Elite Series stops, the Pärnu Openbest 2 of 3400
3150RPM Open, Turku Open, your National Championshipall 3450

Six results counted, and 1,100 points for a perfect season — the DGPT's own figure, which is the arithmetic check that the table above has been read correctly. The counting caps are the interesting part rather than the points: category 1 is the only place to bank 250 and it can only be banked once, so winning both the European Championships and the European Open is worth no more than winning either, while category 3 discards nothing and gives a player who cannot travel a floor to build on. The 2028 card bands are published too: a Full Tour Card to the top 6 MPO / top 3 FPO, a EuroTour Card through 24th / 12th.

What is assumed there. Only the win value of each category is published, so the shape of the curve below first place is ours — the DGPT's own per-place curve, scaled so first pays the category's number. That scaling lands category 3 on exactly the Elite Series curve and categories 2 and 1 on the DGPT+ and Playoff multipliers, which is a good sign that it is how the table was built, but it is still an inference. The national championship weekend is modelled as parallel single-country fields, which is what the eligibility rules describe — entry restricted by citizenship or residence, one championship per player — with the smallest federations pooled into a single regional championship, itself an eligible category, so that a lone entrant cannot win a national title unopposed every season.

The EuroTour tab's real blind spot is its roster. It is built from players who appear in the 2026 DGPT World Standings, which is where the ratings and the attendance rates come from. A European who plays only domestic events has no row in it, and in a real EuroTour standings a good number of them certainly would. At the three stops that are open to the whole tour — the European Open and both European Elite Series events — the model at least races the table against an outside field drawn from the rest of the standings, so a European's finish there is set against everyone who shows up rather than against Europeans alone. Nowhere else can it do that. Read the table as "how the players we can see would fare", not as the field.

Displacement is not modelled. A European who has already earned a Full Tour Card through the DGPT World Standings passes their EuroTour Card down the standings, and a Full Tour Card winner may elect to take a EuroTour Card instead if they expect less US travel. Both rules only ever push cards further down, so for a player sitting just outside a band the odds here are a floor.

The What-if tab is the 2027 projection asked backwards. Instead of "where does this player finish", it asks "what would it take" — set a rating and a number of DGPT, DGPT+, major and JomezPro starts, and it reports every column the 2027 table carries for a player who does not exist. It runs in your browser, 8,000 simulated seasons every time you move a slider, against a summary of the projection the daily refresh ships alongside it: the season totals sitting at each standings position, the two playoff qualification lines, the odds a given finish at a playoff event wins a Cup place, and the ratings you would meet at each Cup seed.

What it adds to the assumptions above. Four things, and the tab lists all four. The field is frozen: you are added to the standings rather than swapped into them, so every point you win is a point nobody else loses — which flatters you most where the race is tightest, and at the very top means you are racing a player the model still has winning the season. Which events you play is a fresh random draw from each class every simulated season, so a player who only entered the stops that suit them would do better than this. The doubles championship is left off entirely, because it needs a partner and 2027 pairings cannot be known. And the Cup is played out against the mean rating at each seed rather than against a distribution of who holds it. The two playoff events are deliberately not sliders: you qualify for those on points or you do not, and watching that happen as you drag the others is most of the point.

The 2027 and EuroTour tables are rebuilt once a day off the current standings, at 20,000 simulated seasons rather than 100,000 — the assumptions above are worth far more uncertainty than the extra runs would buy back. The What-if tab reads the same daily bundle and does its own arithmetic as you move the controls.

Sep 22A gap to the cutline now comes with a price, and there is a head-to-head panel. "137 short" is a true number that nobody can act on. On the What's left tab, the cutline chart now also draws the line where it lands in a kind season and in a cruel one — its 10th and 90th percentiles either side of the median — and a table under it prices all three as a finishing place at the last event on the calendar: the worst place that still carries a player over each line. The reason the same gap costs two players different finishes is the 2026 counting caps. A result is only worth what it beats, so a player whose best two playoff finishes are already banked keeps only the difference, and the arithmetic has to run through their own pools rather than off the points table. Below it, Head to head takes any two players still in contention and answers the other question: not how far from the line, but how far from each other. Pick a pair and it walks the leader's weekend down the points curve — a win, a podium, a 20th, dead last — and for each one gives the finish the chaser needs and how many places clear of them that is. The margin is not a fixed number, because the curve is brutally top-heavy: catching someone who finishes 3rd is a different task from catching the same player finishing 30th, and against a big enough lead the panel says so outright rather than printing a column of dashes. Both are exact arithmetic on the published curve, not a simulation — they say what has to happen, not how likely it is, which is what the rest of the tab is for.
Sep 20The event-odds tab now shows who went out, who came back, and when. Under the win-probability chart there is a tally of skulls — one for each player who, at that moment, took none of the ten thousand simulated tournaments. That is the strongest thing this model can say about anybody: with 10,000 runs it is not "unlikely", it is the resolution floor. They are sparse on Thursday and a wall of them on Sunday afternoon. Climbing back out takes ten times that, 0.1%, which is what the rest of the tab means by alive — do it and you get a phoenix. The gap between the two thresholds is deliberate: on a single one, a player sitting on the floor flickers between marks every few minutes, and across the races on file the gap holds the worst case to two deaths for any one player while keeping the bird genuinely rare, about one an event. The table below is no longer cut at 0.1% either — it carries the whole field, everyone still alive first and then the players who have fallen through, most recently out at the top, with a new Out column giving the moment each one went. A player who was never above the floor reads "never" rather than a time, because being recorded for your place on the leaderboard is not the same as having been in it. The thresholds are each player's own odds, not the fork chart's lines: those are scores, and a player slips under one without playing a bad hole whenever the field ahead of them birdies.

Sep 19The fork line now sets its lines by risk. Yesterday's version cut them on each player's own win probability — the worst score still holding better than 10%, better than 1%, and so on. That reads like a calibrated statement and is not one: how much of the field sits below such a line depends on how many players clear the bar, so measured across this season's recorded updates the "10%" line carried a median 13% chance of being wrong, and 47% at its worst. A label that swings four-fold behind a fixed number is not telling you anything. The lines are now quantiles of the winner's score instead. Walk the board down from the leader adding up win probability, and stop once you have covered 80%, 95% or 99.5% of it; what is left below is the chance the call is wrong, so each line is labelled by its own error rate — 1 in 5, 1 in 20, 1 in 200. Over a 24-event season, and checking once an event, the 1-in-20 line is being wrong about once a season and the 1-in-200 line about once a decade. Scrubbing the chart now also reports how many players are still in it at each tolerance. The reading is unchanged in the one way that matters — the guarantee runs downward, and above a line nothing is promised — and nothing about the model or any projected number changed.

Sep 4The what-if's cutline is now weighted correctly. Expanding a row runs that player's season against 25,000 recorded cutlines, and the cutline they actually have to beat is the one made of other players — which is the 28th-place total in the seasons they missed the cut and the 29th in the seasons they made it. That was being handled by averaging the two cutlines and testing against the average, which is not the same thing: a season total landing between the two counted as a certain miss rather than a partial one, and that gap is exactly where a bubble player sits. It now weighs the two outcomes instead of the two thresholds. Today the difference is smaller than the replay's own noise, and it stops being small in a one-event-left finish. Still an approximation in one respect worth naming: a player who wins takes points that would have gone to the players the cutline is made of, and no row expander can re-run the whole field to account for it.
